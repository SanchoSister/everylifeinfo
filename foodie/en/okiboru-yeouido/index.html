🍜Seoul Foodie: Okiboru Yeouido — NYC's No.1 Ramen, Tsukemen

A real-visit review (visited late July 2026) of Okiboru's first Korean location, timed just outside the notorious weekday lunch rush.

Address1F, World Vision Building, 77-1 Yeouinaru-ro, Yeongdeungpo-gu, Seoul (오키보루)
HoursMon–Fri 10:30–21:00 (break 14:30–17:00), Sat 11:00–20:00, closed Sunday
ParkingAvailable at the World Vision building, likely paid
MapOpen in Google Maps

About this place

  • Okiboru started as a tsukemen specialist in Manhattan, New York, and landed on the New York Times' 2025 list of 100 best restaurants — The Infatuation also ranked it the No.1 ramen spot in NYC. This Yeouido location, opened in March 2026, is Okiboru's first branch in Korea.
  • Enter through a separate entrance on the side of the World Vision building, past what looks like a parking area. It's close to IFC Mall and The Hyundai Seoul, making it an easy add-on if you're already visiting either mall.
  • The space blends Japanese ramen-shop mood with a hip, relaxed feel and a slight New York edge — not overdone. Plenty of bar/counter seating for solo diners, plus a coat rack and a baby chair.
  • We went right at opening to dodge the wait — it's said to get long during weekday lunch peak, so an "opening run" is worth it if you want to skip the line.

Menu & what we ordered

  • Tsukemen is the signature, alongside spicy tsukemen, yuzu tsukemen, and regular ramen options like Hakata paitan and tori paitan.
  • Tsukemen is a distinct style of ramen where cold (soba-like) noodles are dipped into a separate hot broth, rather than served submerged in soup.
  • Extra noodles, wari-soup (broth dilution), and rice are all free refills — good news for big eaters.
  • As a first-timer, we ordered the basic tsukemen and a gyoza set.

The food

  • Being an early customer meant a bit of a wait for the food, and the "NYC's No.1 ramen" title had built up our expectations quite a bit going in.
  • Tsukemen was first devised in Higashi-Ikebukuro, Tokyo, in 1955 and has since spread across Japan as one of the two pillars of Japanese noodle culture alongside regular ramen. The dipping sauce is designed much more concentrated than regular ramen broth, since dipping the noodles naturally dilutes it to the right seasoning — meaning the sauce alone, undiluted, is extremely salty by design. Because you dip cold or room-temperature noodles into hot sauce, keeping the sauce hot is key to the whole experience.
  • Staff recommended squeezing lime onto the noodles first before dipping — a distinctive touch here, adding a slightly tangy brightness to the noodles themselves.
  • The noodles came topped with a generous pile of scallions, two pieces of menma (fermented bamboo shoots), a cute little fish cake, and two large chunks of chashu.
  • The broth was a rich, heavy pork-based dipping sauce. Dipping the noodles let it soak in nicely — but with the AC running strong for summer, the hot broth cooled faster than expected. Since sauce temperature is everything for tsukemen, the flavor suffered a bit as it cooled over the meal.
  • The chashu was very tender and the menma was good too, but the overall flavor was solid rather than spectacular — less impactful than tsukemen we'd had in Fukuoka. Not bad at all, just a bit more ordinary than the "NYC's No.1" title had us expecting.
  • The gyoza (two pieces in the set) was honestly unremarkable — eating gyoza at ramen shop after ramen shop really makes you appreciate how good Bibigo's packaged gyoza is by comparison.

The real highlight: the ending

  • After finishing the noodles, you get free wari-soup (a clean, unseasoned broth) and rice. Mixing the wari-soup into the leftover dipping sauce dilutes it to just the right soup strength.
  • Adding rice to that turns it into something very close to a Korean pork bone soup (dwaeji-gukbap) ending — and honestly, this rich, savory pork-broth-meets-rice finale was the most satisfying part of the whole meal. If you order tsukemen here, don't skip the wari-soup + rice ending.

Overall

  • The "NYC's No.1" title raised expectations that the food itself didn't quite match — the taste was solid but more ordinary than expected, and less impactful than tsukemen we'd had in Fukuoka. The AC cooling the sauce too quickly was a minor letdown, but the wari-soup + rice ending is a strong recommend. If you want to avoid the weekday lunch wait, try a weekday dinner, weekend, or an opening run.
